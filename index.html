Finding a course on Coursera should be easy. Is it?

A heuristic review and a usability test of coursera.org, done by a college student, with college students.

Ayub Sanchez Yaqen · September 2026

The project

Why Coursera

Why I picked it

I use Coursera. When a skill I need is not in my classes, this is where I end up. It is also a product where the choice you make costs real money and real time, so a confusing page is not a small thing.

What it does and who uses it

Coursera hosts online courses, Specializations, Professional Certificates and degrees from universities and companies like Google and IBM. People search for a topic, compare options and enroll. Watching is often free. Certificates usually cost money or a Coursera Plus subscription. It is used by students, career changers and employees whose company pays. I focused on college students who want a career-related skill.

Who and why

The user, the top tasks, and what Coursera wants

Target user

A college student, or someone who was in college in the last year, who wants to learn a new skill for school, work or a career.

Top tasks

  • Search for a topic
  • Filter by level, duration or product type
  • Compare a course with a certificate
  • Check cost, level, schedule and time
  • Start enrolling

Business goal

Coursera earns when someone enrolls and pays, per certificate or through Coursera Plus. Every moment a student is unsure what is free, what costs money, or which option is which, is a moment Coursera can lose the sale or the trust.

What I wanted to learn

Three questions

1

Can a student find a beginner-friendly course on a topic they actually want to learn?

2

Do they understand the difference between a standalone course, a Specialization and a Professional Certificate?

3

How easy is it to filter, compare, check the cost and time, and start enrolling?

These are the steps between a student and actually learning something. When they break, the student gives up or pays for the wrong thing.

What I did

Two methods, one topic

Heuristic review

August 2026 · Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics

I walked through coursera.org the way a student would, searching for data analytics, opening courses, and trying to enroll. At each step I checked the screen against the ten heuristics and noted where it broke one.

Usability test

Unmoderated · think-aloud · Lookback · 4 participants

Participants opened coursera.org on their own computer and worked through five tasks while talking out loud. After each task they rated how easy it was from 1 to 10. At the end they answered four short questions.

Who took part

Current college student, or in college within the past year Has searched for a class or learning resource online before Wants to learn a new skill for school, work or career

Recruited through classmates, friends and student groups.

What Maria said

Review of my test plan

  • One task per screen, and tell people when to stop.
  • Give a real situation instead of saying filter. Make sure the thing you ask for actually exists.
  • Ask for two concrete things, like cost and time. Stop before any personal information.
Then basically what they're doing is they're just acting like a tester, like a QA person, rather than being themselves.Maria Rosala

The test

The five tasks

Find something you'd want to learn

Think of something you've been meaning to learn. Could be for work, could be a hobby. Spend up to 5 minutes looking around Coursera for a course on it that you'd actually want to take. Once you've found one that looks right for you, stop.

Stay on the same topic. Imagine you're starting from scratch and you only have a few hours a week. Find a beginner level course on your topic that you could finish in a month or less. If nothing fits exactly, pick the closest thing and tell us why.

Find two courses on your topic that both look decent. For each one, figure out whether you'd get a certificate at the end and what it would take to earn it, like paying a fee, passing quizzes or finishing a project. Say which one is the easier path to a certificate.

Go with the course you like best so far. Find out what it would cost you and about how long it would take you to finish. Say both out loud.

Imagine you've decided to take that course. Start enrolling. Get as far as you can, but stop before it asks for any personal details or payment info.

After each task: On a scale of 1 to 10, how easy or difficult was that?

At the end: What do you think about using Coursera for this? Was anything particularly easy or difficult? Is there anything you would improve?

Results · part one

What the heuristic review found

Three issues stood out, and all three sit right on the path a student takes from a search to an enroll button. Two of them are about clutter. One is about a word Coursera uses that does not mean what students think it means.

1 · Crowded search results

Aesthetic and minimalist design

2 · AI chat covers the course page

Aesthetic and minimalist design

3 · Enroll for free does not mean free

Match between system and real world
Finding 1 Aesthetic and minimalist design Medium

Crowded search results

Search for data analytics and the page shows an AI overview, recommended course cards, suggested questions, an open AI chat panel and a cookie banner, all at once. They compete for attention and push the actual results below the fold.

Recommendation

Show fewer things at once. Keep the AI chat closed by default and let people open it. Make the cookie banner smaller so the results, not the banner, own the screen.

Finding 2 Aesthetic and minimalist design Medium

The AI chat follows you onto the course page

Open a course and the chat panel is still open, taking a big slice of the screen. The course information gets harder to scan, and you have to close the chat before you can read comfortably.

Recommendation

Minimize the chat when a course page opens. A small Ask Coursera button is enough, and the panel expands only when someone taps it.

Finding 3 Match between system and real world High

Enroll for free does not mean what students think

The button says Enroll for free. The same page says the course is included with Coursera Plus. Nowhere does it say whether the certificate is free, whether only part of the course is free, or whether a subscription will be asked for later. For a student, free means free.

Recommendation

Say what free includes before asking for an account. Show the price, the trial terms and what free enrollment gets you, and change the button to something honest like Create an account to see enrollment options.
