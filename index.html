Finding a course on Coursera should be easy. Is it?

A heuristic review and a usability test of coursera.org, run by a college student with four college students.

Ayub Sanchez Yaqen · September 2026

The project

I picked Coursera because a confusing page here costs students real money

It is the site I use when a class does not cover a skill I need

When a skill I need is not in my classes, Coursera is where I end up. Every choice here costs money and time, so a confusing page is not a small thing.

Coursera sells courses, Specializations, Professional Certificates and degrees

The content comes from universities and companies like Google and IBM. Watching is often free. Certificates usually cost money or a Coursera Plus subscription. Students, career changers and employees whose company pays all use it. I focused on college students who want a career-related skill.

Who and why

Coursera earns when a student enrolls and pays, so every unclear step costs a sale

The user is a college student learning a skill for work

A college student, or someone who was in college in the last year, who wants to learn a new skill for school, work or a career.

Five things they must get through before they can learn anything

  • Search for a topic
  • Filter by level, duration or product type
  • Compare a course with a certificate
  • Check cost, level, schedule and time
  • Start enrolling

The business needs enrollments and trust, in that order

Coursera earns per certificate or through Coursera Plus. A student who cannot tell what is free, what costs money, or which option is which either leaves, or enrolls and feels tricked later. Both cost Coursera.

What I wanted to learn

Three questions, all sitting on the path from a search to an enroll button

1

Can a student find a beginner-friendly course on a topic they actually want to learn?

2

Do they understand the difference between a standalone course, a Specialization and a Professional Certificate?

3

How easy is it to filter, compare, check the cost and time, and start enrolling?

These are the steps between a student and actually learning something. When they break, the student gives up or pays for the wrong thing.

What I did and why

A heuristic review to find likely problems, then real students to see which ones actually bite

The heuristic review found the candidates

August 2026 · Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics

I walked through coursera.org the way a student would: searched data analytics, opened courses, tried to enroll. At each step I checked the screen against the ten heuristics. It is fast and cheap, but it is one person's opinion.

An unmoderated usability test checked them against real people

Think-aloud · Lookback · 4 college students

I wanted real students, not me and not an AI, going through the design. Participants opened coursera.org on their own computer, did five tasks while talking out loud, rated each task from 1 to 10, then answered four questions. Unmoderated, so people could do it on their own time with nobody in the room nudging them.

Participants had to match the user I care about

Current college student, or in college within the past year Has searched for a class or learning resource online before Wants to learn a new skill for school, work or career

Recruited through classmates, friends and student groups.

What Maria said

Review of my test plan

  • One task per screen, and tell people when to stop.
  • Give a real situation instead of saying filter. Make sure the thing you ask for actually exists.
  • Ask for two concrete things, like cost and time. Stop before any personal information.
Then basically what they're doing is they're just acting like a tester, like a QA person, rather than being themselves.Maria Rosala

The test

Five tasks that follow the real path: find, fit, compare, price, enroll

Find something you'd want to learn

Think of something you've been meaning to learn. Could be for work, could be a hobby. Spend up to 5 minutes looking around Coursera for a course on it that you'd actually want to take. Once you've found one that looks right for you, stop.

Why this task: it is Coursera's front door. If search and browse cannot get a student to something they would actually take, nothing else matters.

Stay on the same topic. Imagine you're starting from scratch and you only have a few hours a week. Find a beginner level course on your topic that you could finish in a month or less. If nothing fits exactly, pick the closest thing and tell us why.

Why this task: it needs the level and duration filters, but nobody is told to filter. I wanted to see whether the filters get found on their own.

Find two courses on your topic that both look decent. For each one, figure out whether you'd get a certificate at the end and what it would take to earn it, like paying a fee, passing quizzes or finishing a project. Say which one is the easier path to a certificate.

Why this task: course, Specialization and Professional Certificate have to make sense, and people have to understand what earns a certificate. That is the thing Coursera sells.

Go with the course you like best so far. Find out what it would cost you and about how long it would take you to finish. Say both out loud.

Why this task: cost and time are the two numbers that decide an enrollment. If they are hard to find, students guess or leave.

Imagine you've decided to take that course. Start enrolling. Get as far as you can, but stop before it asks for any personal details or payment info.

Why this task: it walks up to the paywall. This is where free has to mean free, and where an account is asked for before any price is shown.

After each task: On a scale of 1 to 10, how easy or difficult was that?

At the end: What do you think about using Coursera for this? Was anything particularly easy or difficult? Is there anything you would improve?

Results · part one · heuristic review

Three issues, and the biggest one is that free does not mean free

Two are clutter that pushes the courses off the screen. One is a single word on the most important button on the site. I ranked the wording issue highest because it hits trust and revenue, not just comfort.

Finding 1 Aesthetic and minimalist design Medium

The search page shows five things at once, and the courses lose

Search data analytics and you get an AI overview, recommended cards, suggested questions, an open AI chat panel and a cookie banner, all at once. They compete for attention and push the actual results below the fold. A student who came to compare courses has to clear things away first.

Recommendation: show the results first, everything else on request

Keep the AI chat closed by default behind a small Ask Coursera button. Shrink the cookie banner to one line. Collapse the AI overview to its first sentence with a Show more. Result cards should own the first screen, because that is where students decide whether to keep going.

Finding 2 Aesthetic and minimalist design Medium

The AI chat stays open on the course page and hides a third of it

Open a course and the chat panel is still open, taking a big slice of the screen. The course information gets harder to scan, and you have to close the chat before you can read comfortably. Nobody asked for it to be there.

Recommendation: minimize the chat when a course page opens

Replace the open panel with a small Ask Coursera pill in the corner. Expand it only when someone taps it, and remember the choice. The page gets its width back and the chat is still one tap away.

Finding 3 Match between system and real world High

Enroll for free does not mean free, and students find out after they sign up

The button says Enroll for free. The same page says the course is included with Coursera Plus. Nowhere does it say whether the certificate is free, whether only part of the course is free, or whether a subscription will be asked for later. For a student, free means free. Finding out otherwise after creating an account feels sly, and that is a reputation problem, not a layout problem.

Recommendation: say what free includes before asking for an account

Put the price, the trial terms and what free gets you right under the button: free to audit, $49 a month for the certificate after a 7-day trial, financial aid available. Change the button to something honest like Create an account to see enrollment options. Fewer surprised students means fewer refunds, fewer angry reviews, and more people who come back.
